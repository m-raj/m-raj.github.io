Estimating local strain fields in two-phase composites with a UNet

Mayank Raj, Sanket Thakre, Ratna Kumar Annabattula, Anand K Kanjarla · IIT Madras
Integrating Materials and Manufacturing Innovation 10 (2021) 444–460

Paper (DOI)

Where a load concentrates inside a material decides where cracks and voids start. Finding those local fields normally takes a finite-element or FFT simulation for every new microstructure, which is far too slow for searching a design space. This project trains a UNet, a convolutional network first built for image segmentation, to map a whole 3D microstructure to its full strain field in one forward pass. It then uses statistics to explain where and why the network makes mistakes.

The dataset: synthetic two-phase microstructures

Each microstructure starts as 3D uniform random noise. A Gaussian filter smooths it; a wide filter along one axis stretches features along that axis. Thresholding then sets the volume fraction of the soft phase. Six filter shapes give six morphology classes, from small spheres to needles. How one sample is made (class 4)

Each cube was meshed in ABAQUS with periodic boundary conditions and stretched by a uniaxial strain of 0.001 along x. Both phases are linear elastic. The network's target is the resulting maximum principal strain in every voxel.

The model: a 3D-aware UNet

An earlier approach (Yang et al., 2019) predicts one voxel at a time from the small cube around it, so neighbouring voxels repeat almost all of the same work. Here the whole microstructure goes in and the whole strain field comes out. The network uses only convolutions, so it has far fewer parameters and parallelises well.

Loss

Mean absolute error on standardised strain

Optimiser

Adam, learning rate 10⁻³ cut by 0.9 after 10 flat epochs, weight decay 10⁻⁶

Training

Batches of 200, 1200 epochs (loss levels off by ~600); 75% train / 25% validation

Framework

PyTorch, with ABAQUS FE simulations as ground truth

Why some microstructures are harder

R² is about the same in every class, but the absolute error roughly doubles for the 30% microstructures. A variance decomposition explains why: those strain fields simply vary more. About 40% of all strain variance comes from the gap between the two phases' average strains. That gap grows with stiffness contrast and scales as v(1−v), so it is largest at a 50/50 mix.

The errors are also biased. The network under-predicts the largest strains, almost all of which sit in the soft phase, whose strain distribution has a long right-hand tail. Below is the average error (true − predicted) in the soft phase for each class.

Does a model trained on one morphology transfer?

Separate models were trained on single classes and tested on all the others. The pattern is clear. Morphologies 1–5 partly transfer to each other, but class 6 (needles along the loading direction) stands apart. Changing the volume fraction breaks transfer almost completely: a model trained at 30% can score far below zero on 10% data. The lesson for building datasets is to sample the high-variance classes and the volume fractions you care about, rather than spreading samples evenly.

Takeaways

  • A UNet maps a whole 3D microstructure to its whole strain field at once, reaching R² ≈ 94% on unseen samples and avoiding the repeated work of voxel-by-voxel models.
  • Error is driven by the variance of the target field: higher stiffness contrast and a more even phase mix make it harder.
  • The model is biased low on extreme strains in the soft phase, where the distribution has a long tail.
  • Transfer across morphologies is partial, and across volume fractions it is poor, so datasets should over-sample the hard cases.
  • The model is fast enough to sit inside an optimisation loop for inverse microstructure design.
