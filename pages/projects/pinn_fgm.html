Physics-informed neural networks for thermo-mechanics of functionally graded materials

Mayank Raj, Pramod Kumbhar, Ratna Kumar Annabattula · IIT Madras
arXiv preprint 2111.10751 (2022)

Paper (arXiv)

A physics-informed neural network (PINN) solves a differential equation without any training data. The network takes a point's coordinates and outputs its displacement or temperature. Training minimises the system's energy: strain energy plus thermal energy, minus the work done by loads. Whatever minimises that energy is the solution. This project used PINNs, for the first time, on coupled thermo-mechanics in functionally graded materials, whose stiffness and conductivity vary smoothly through the part. It also asked what PINNs get right and what they get wrong.

How an energy-based PINN works

$$\mathcal L=\sum_i w_i\Big[\tfrac12\,\varepsilon^{el}:\mathbf C(\mathbf x_i):\varepsilon^{el}+\tfrac12\,k(\mathbf x_i)\,\nabla T\cdot\nabla T\Big]\;-\;\sum_{\Gamma_t} w'_i\,\mathbf t\cdot\mathbf u,\qquad \varepsilon^{el}=\varepsilon-\alpha\,\Delta T\,\mathbf I$$

Try it: train a PINN in your browser

This is a real 1→5→5→1 tanh network, the same size as in the paper, training on 50 points in a graded bar with stiffness E(x) = 1/(1+x). Pick a problem and press Train. The left plot shows the loss heading to the bar's true minimum energy (dashed). The right plot shows the network's displacement closing in on the exact solution.

Gradients with respect to x are exact (forward-mode). Gradients with respect to the weights use finite differences, which is enough for a 46-parameter net. The paper used TensorFlow's automatic differentiation.

Two lessons from the paper show up here. First, the loss is a physical quantity: it settles at the true minimum energy, which is negative when the load does more work than the bar stores. Second, the boundary wrapper matters. The "bad" wrapper ũ = x² + x²(1−x²)ṽ does hold u(0)=0 and u(1)=1, but it also forces the slope at x = 0 to be zero, which the true solution doesn't have. The loss stalls above the true minimum and the curve misses near the fixed end.

The catch: great displacements, shaky stresses

Displacement and temperature come out at R² above 99% in every case. Stresses come from derivatives of the network output, and they are much less reliable: some drop to 70–90%, and a few go below zero.

A negative R² can look worse than it is. When the true stress is nearly constant, as σ₂₂ is in a plate under uniform tension or σ₁₁ is in a 1D bar, its variance is close to zero. R² divides the error by that variance, so even a tiny error gives a large negative score. The paper flags this: R² is not always the right measure for PINN accuracy.

Takeaways

  • Energy-based PINNs solve coupled thermo-elastic problems with graded properties without meshes or data, reaching R² > 99% for displacement and temperature.
  • The loss converges to the system's true minimum energy, which is useful as a convergence check.
  • Fixed-value wrappers must not secretly constrain slopes. Reaction work is not subtracted when fixed values are built in.
  • Accurate displacements do not guarantee accurate stresses. Derivative quantities are the weak spot, and R² can mislead for nearly uniform fields.
  • Open questions: how to design architectures and activations, consistent stopping rules, transfer learning, and distributed PINNs.
