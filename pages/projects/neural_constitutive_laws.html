A neural-network framework to learn history-dependent constitutive laws

Mayank Raj, Lianghao Cao, Andrew Stuart, Kaushik Bhattacharya · Caltech
Journal of the Mechanics and Physics of Solids 217 (2026) 106807

Paper (DOI) Code on GitHub

Metals deform in ways that depend on their loading history. Simulating that behaviour across scales (FE²) is very expensive, so a common shortcut is to train a neural network to stand in for the fine-scale model. But a plain recurrent network has no reason to respect thermodynamics, stay stable under extreme strain, or give a well-posed boundary-value problem. This project builds a surrogate that is physically consistent by construction: the network learns an energy and a dissipation potential, and stress and internal-variable evolution are derived from them.

The problem: from microstructure to a macroscopic law

The training data come from a crystal-plasticity model of a magnesium polycrystal: a cube with 128 randomly oriented grains, each with basal, prismatic and pyramidal slip systems plus tensile twinning. The cell is driven by random deformation-gradient paths $\mathbf F(t)$, and the Taylor-averaged stress $\mathbf P^\dagger(t)$ is recorded. The goal is the history-dependent map

$$\Psi^\dagger : \{\mathbf F(\tau)\}_{\tau\in(0,t)} \;\longmapsto\; \mathbf P^\dagger(t).$$

The model: energy + dissipation, with internal variables

History is carried by a vector of internal variables $\xi(t)\in\mathbb R^n$ that the network discovers on its own: nobody tells it what they mean. Two potentials drive everything: a stored energy $\mathcal W(\mathbf F,\xi)$ and a dissipation potential $\mathcal D$ (written through its Legendre transform so the dynamics become an explicit ODE):

$$\mathbf P = \frac{\partial \mathcal W(\mathbf F,\xi)}{\partial \mathbf F},\qquad d = -\frac{\partial \mathcal W(\mathbf F,\xi)}{\partial \xi},\qquad \dot\xi = \frac{\partial \mathcal D(d)}{\partial d},\quad \xi(0)=0.$$

Splitting the energy into volume-changing and shape-changing parts, $\mathcal W = \mathcal W_H(\det\mathbf F) + \mathcal W_V(\mathbf K,\xi)$ with $\mathbf K=\mathbf F/(\det \mathbf F)^{1/3}$, turned out to matter a lot: the diagonal stress components are much larger than the shear ones, and learning them together makes training hard.

Physics built into the architecture

Second law of thermodynamics

The dissipation potential is a convex network with its minimum at zero, so the dissipated power is never negative.

Polyconvexity

Energy is convex in $(\mathbf F,\ \mathrm{adj}\,\mathbf F,\ \det\mathbf F)$, the condition behind existence theorems for nonlinear elasticity (Ball, 1976).

Material stability

Analytic growth terms make energy blow up under extreme stretch or as $\det\mathbf F\to0^+$, without disturbing the fit inside the data.

Objectivity

Trained on symmetric $\mathbf F$ only, then extended through the polar decomposition $\mathbf F=\mathbf Q\tilde{\mathbf F}$, so rotating the frame rotates the stress exactly.

A network only learns where it has data. Outside that range it can do anything, including predict that a material can be crushed to a point with finite force. Two hand-built functions fix this: they are nearly flat over the data and grow polynomially outside it.

How many internal variables?

The number of internal variables is not known in advance. Models with $n=1\ldots12$ were trained with identical architecture and data. Error falls steeply up to $n=6$ and then plateaus, so six was chosen.

Identifiability: internal variables are unique up to a linear map

Two networks trained on the same data from different random starts predict the same stress, but their internal variables look different. The paper proves this is the only freedom: under a controllability assumption, any two models with identical stress–strain response have internal variables related by an invertible linear map,

$$\eta(t) = A\,\xi(t),\qquad A\in GL(n,\mathbb R).$$

Conversely, any such $A$ yields an equivalent model by setting $\tilde{\mathcal W}(f,A p)=\mathcal W(f,p)$ and $\tilde{\mathcal D}(A q)=\hat{\mathcal D}(q)$, which keeps convexity. Empirically, a linear regression fitted on training trajectories maps one model's variables onto the other's with about 5% median error on unseen test trajectories.

Speed

Training is a one-off cost of about 24 hours for the coupled energy/dissipation networks and 1 hour for the volumetric network, on a single NVIDIA P100.

Takeaways

  • Building a learned constitutive law from an energy and a dissipation potential gives thermodynamic consistency, stability and objectivity by construction, at 2% error.
  • Splitting the energy into volumetric and deviatoric parts cut the error from 15% to 2%.
  • Learned internal variables are identifiable up to a linear transform. This is proven under assumptions and also seen in experiments beyond them.
  • Open directions: microstructure-dependent and non-local potentials, and whether the map $A$ splits into blocks that line up with isotropic and kinematic hardening.
